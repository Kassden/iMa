TRANSCRIPT

01. OPENING AND THE PURPOSE OF THE TALK
-------------------------------------
[Music]

Professor Yao, thank you very much for inviting me here today.

The title of my talk is "What Are My Odds?" I think that is appropriate.
My luck is actually running rather badly today because I have been chosen
to follow such an erudite and entertaining lecture by the previous speaker.
Hopefully, I will be able to impart a little interesting information.

The subject is historical and modern efforts to win at games of chance.
The things I will discuss are:

1. How gambling, and a desire to win at gambling, inspired the scientific
   study of probability.
2. Three key mathematical concepts that emerged during that early study
   and describe the majority of gambling phenomena.
3. Some modern contributions of mathematicians to solving present-day
   gambling problems.


02. THE CHEVALIER DE MERE'S DICE PROBLEM
-------------------------------------
Everything begins with gambling, or at least the important modern history
begins in 1654.

There was a well-known gambler in Paris named the Chevalier de Mere. He
was perplexed by a seemingly inconsistent result in some popular games
of chance played at the time.

The illustration on the left is not really the Chevalier. It is a person
in the costume of the time, so that may be what he looked like.

His particular question concerned betting on rolls of dice:

"If it is profitable to wager that a six will appear within four rolls of
one die, why is it not profitable to wager that double sixes will appear
within 24 rolls of two dice?"

Four rolls represent two-thirds of the six possible outcomes of one die.
Twenty-four rolls also represent two-thirds of the 36 possible combinations
of two dice. He thought those situations should be the same.

[Editorial clarification: the ratios 4/6 and 24/36 explain de Mere's
intuition, not the correct probability calculation. Assuming fair,
independent rolls:

  P(at least one six in four rolls) = 1 - (5/6)^4
                                   = approximately 0.51775.

  P(at least one double six in 24 rolls) = 1 - (35/36)^24
                                        = approximately 0.49140.

Their expectations differ for an even-money wager. These numerical
calculations are editorial additions, not equations transcribed from a
supplied slide.]

De Mere took his question to his friend Blaise Pascal. As we all know,
Pascal was one of the greats of mathematics, so de Mere was very lucky
to have him as a friend.

Stimulated by the question, Pascal began a now-famous correspondence with
another mathematician of the time, Pierre de Fermat, known for Fermat's
Last Theorem. They were friends.

Historians of mathematics have examined these letters for many years.
It was evident to the two men that no existing theory adequately described
a solution to de Mere's problem.

What resulted was a chain of correspondence extending for some years.
Important ideas forming a foundation for probability theory resulted
from it.


03. THE FIRST CONCEPT: PROBABILITY
--------------------------------
The three key concepts relevant to practical gambling problems are:

1. Probability.
2. Mathematical expectation.
3. The law of large numbers.

Early work on probability dealt with games such as dice rolling and coin
tossing, where the game could be broken down into equally likely outcomes.

A die has six sides. If you roll one die, there are six possible outcomes.
Any one of the six numbers can come up. The probability of a particular
side is the number of outcomes that make the event true divided by the
number of equally likely possible outcomes.

For example, the chance of rolling a five is one out of six. There are
six possible outcomes and one corresponds to success, so the probability
is 1/6.

A further extension is to express probability as the limit of the number
of successes divided by the number of trials. Looking at the equation,
its form is similar: the limit of an observed frequency can also be
thought of as the probability.

[Editorial qualification: convergence of observed frequencies requires
appropriate assumptions about the repeated trials. This is a frequency
interpretation, not an unconditional guarantee for arbitrary data.]


04. THE SECOND CONCEPT: MATHEMATICAL EXPECTATION
----------------------------------------------
The second big idea relevant to gambling is mathematical expectation.
This idea was attributed to the Dutch mathematician Christiaan Huygens,
pictured on the right.

Mathematical expectation is a weighted average of a random variable.
The equation at the bottom expresses the expectation of X as the sum of
its possible values, each multiplied by its probability:

  E[X] = p_1*x_1 + p_2*x_2 + ...

This is a weighted average outcome.

It is important for gambling because the result of a gamble is a random
variable that can assume different values, usually winning or losing.

For the return on a particular bet, there is a probability p of a profit
and a complementary probability 1-p of a loss:

  Expected net return = p*(net profit if successful)
                     + (1-p)*(net loss if unsuccessful).

To make that clear, I have worked it out for a bet on roulette.
There are 38 possible numbers on the wheel. If you win, you receive a net
profit of 35 units. If you lose, you lose the one unit you bet.

You have a 1/38 chance of winning a profit of 35 and a 37/38 chance of
losing your dollar:

  E[X] = (1/38)*35 + (37/38)*(-1)
       = -2/38
       = approximately -0.0526.

Your expectation is to lose about 0.0526 of the amount staked. It is a
negative-expectation bet.

[Editorial clarification: this example refers to a 38-pocket roulette
wheel. It is not a description of every roulette variant.]

Another important property is that expectation is additive. When you
make a series of bets, each has an expectation. The overall mean result
is the sum of the individual expectations:

  E[X_1 + X_2 + ... + X_n] = E[X_1] + E[X_2] + ... + E[X_n].

This leads to rule number one:

"The only way to achieve a long-term expected profit in gambling is to
make a series of bets that have a net positive expectation."

[Editorial clarification: linearity of expectation does not require
independence. Dependence still matters greatly for variance, drawdowns
and simultaneous portfolio sizing.]


05. THE THIRD CONCEPT: THE LAW OF LARGE NUMBERS
---------------------------------------------
Gambling typically involves repeated trials: bets at the races, hands
at the blackjack table, or plays at roulette.

Independent repeated trials were studied carefully by Jacob Bernoulli,
one of the Bernoulli family. Such trials are often called Bernoulli
trials in his honor.

The formula below is the binomial distribution. It gives the probability
of a particular number of successes in a given series of trials.

[Editorial mathematical notation for the distribution referred to:

  P(K=k) = C(n,k)*p^k*(1-p)^(n-k),

for n independent Bernoulli trials with a common success probability p.
The slide itself was not supplied.]

As the number of trials increases, the ratio of successes to trials
converges stochastically to the expected result. In the graph, the
proportion of successes clusters around 0.5.

Putting the three concepts together:

- Expressing the chance of an event as a probability allows the
  mathematical analysis of a wager.
- The additive property of expectation enables calculation of the
  overall expected result of a series of wagers.
- The law of large numbers explains convergence of average observed
  results toward their expectation.


06. SHORT-RUN RESULTS: 100 TRIALS
-------------------------------
Here is a chart showing how one might fare at a game of chance.
The solid dark line just above the x-axis is the expected result.

Imagine a slightly biased coin. Instead of a 50/50 split, suppose it
comes up heads 51% of the time and tails 49% of the time. You win one
dollar on heads and lose one dollar on tails.

The number of trials runs along the x-axis and your result is on the
y-axis. The expected mean result creeps upward. After 100 trials,
you would expect to be a couple of units ahead.

More important is the spread of possible outcomes. The blue lines show
one standard deviation and the outer pink dotted lines show two
standard deviations.

The chart describes what can happen over 100 trials of a nearly even
coin-flipping game with a narrow player advantage. Your mean result is
slightly positive, but your actual result can readily be 10 or 20 units
above or below it.

This is what people typically face. In a casino, the expectation is
usually slightly negative instead. You could still walk in and play
for an hour or 100 trials, then leave winning a lot or losing a lot.
That is part of what people go to a casino for.


07. MEDIUM- AND LONG-RUN RESULTS
-------------------------------
Now consider the medium run: the same game over 10,000 trials.
The expectation is much larger, and the standard-deviation bands have
shifted relative to the x-axis. The chart shows an expected positive
return of about 100 units.

Most of the probable return is above the x-axis. You would need to be
roughly a standard deviation below expectation to lose overall.
Approximately five-sixths of the time you would be ahead in the
illustrated situation.

Going to an even longer run, perhaps 100,000 plays, the expected result
in the chart is about 1,000 units ahead. Even the lower two-standard-
deviation line is above zero.

This illustrates the law of large numbers applied to gambling: as play
continues, the percentage result approaches the expected result.

I originally prepared these charts for a talk about a winning gambling
system. For a typical casino gambler, the charts would be reversed.

After an hour you could be winning or losing, with a slightly negative
expected result. After 100 hours, perhaps 10,000 blackjack hands in
Macau, you would be much more likely to be behind, though you might
still be ahead. Over 100,000 trials, perhaps 1,000 hours in a year for
a heavy gambler, the probability of being ahead would be very small.

That is a warning about playing negative-expectation games.

[Editorial corrections and qualifications:

2. The transcript uses words such as "guaranteed" and "definitely" for

   on the trial process and assumptions.
3. The probability of eventual or finite-horizon success must not be

   correlated horse-racing market.]


08. MODERN CONTRIBUTIONS TO GAMBLING PROBLEMS
-------------------------------------------
After those theoretical preliminaries, let us consider modern
contributions of mathematicians to previously unsolved gambling problems.

Many basic problems were solved in the 17th century. Beginning with
Pascal and Fermat in 1654, important ideas and mathematical machinery
developed within the following decades.

There have nevertheless been occasional new theoretical developments,
including some in the last 50 years. Increasingly, computer-based
techniques have also been used by gamblers and researchers to find
winning systems for games once thought immune to such efforts.


09. THE KELLY CRITERION: HOW MUCH CAPITAL TO RISK
----------------------------------------------
The first example is an analytical result by J. L. Kelly.
He was a researcher at Bell Laboratories in the 1950s, working on
information transmission. He realized that a finding could be applied
to gambling.

The practical question was:

"What fraction of his capital should a gambler risk on each play?"

A gambler generally has finite capital. Expectation and the law of
large numbers do not, by themselves, specify how much to risk.
Kelly's result addresses that question and is widely used by
professional gamblers.

The Kelly criterion defines an exponential rate of capital growth.
Its derivation is beyond the scope of this lecture, but it gives
simple results in some cases.

Consider an even-money coin game with probability p of winning.
You win a dollar on heads and lose a dollar on tails. If p=0.51,
the optimal fraction under the stated Kelly model is:

  f = 2*p - 1
    = 0.02.

With $1,000 of capital, that would mean $20 on the first play.
As capital grows or shrinks, recalculate the stake to remain 2% of
the current bankroll.

[Editorial arithmetic correction: the supplied transcript first says
$1,000, then says "$10,000" while retaining a $20 stake. Two percent
of $1,000 is $20; two percent of $10,000 is $200. The internally
consistent $1,000 example is used above. This is a substantive
numerical correction, not merely a spelling change.]

[Editorial scope: the formula above is for a single even-money bet
under the stated assumptions. It is not the general simultaneous-
ticket, uncertain-payout, pari-mutuel Kelly solution.]


10. BLACKJACK AND THE USE OF COMPUTERS
------------------------------------
The second example is a practical rather than theoretical advance:
blackjack.

I assume most of you know the game. A player receives two cards;
the dealer has a visible card and a hidden card. The object is to
get as close as possible to a total of 21 without exceeding it.
It involves both skill and chance because the player chooses
whether to take additional cards.

It had been popular in casinos since the late 1800s, but had resisted
solution. The rules were well defined, yet dealing from a deck
created a large number of discrete card-order dependencies.
Writing out and testing all the possibilities by hand would require
millions of combinations.

When digital computers became available for pursuits such as card
games in the early 1960s, Edward Thorp decided to use a computer to
determine an optimal strategy. He worked in conjunction with people
at IBM, and in 1962 published "Beat the Dealer."

That book was a watershed for professional gambling and mathematical
interest in gambling. It showed that a mathematical strategy could
produce a positive expected return in a popular casino game.

That is how I got into the game. I read the book, quit what I was doing
- attending university - moved to Las Vegas, and became a professional
gambler. It worked and generated a lot of interest.

The important technique was using technology to enumerate possibilities.
The computer assisted an arduous calculation rather than providing
a completely new mathematical theory.

Computers are now so fast that many blackjack questions are answered
with simulators instead of detailed enumeration. Encode the rules,
run millions or billions of hands, and tabulate the results to
estimate the house/player advantage or compare strategies.
It is a brute-force calculation method.

[Editorial qualification: this is the speaker's historical account,
not a claim that every present-day blackjack game offers a player
advantage, or that simulation proves every property without statistical
error and correct rule/model assumptions.]


11. HORSE RACING: MODELLING REAL-WORLD PERFORMANCE
------------------------------------------------
The last example is nearest and dearest to my heart: horse racing.

I would say this form of gambling has inspired especially serious and
sophisticated applications of mathematical techniques. There is a
great deal of money in racing worldwide, particularly in Hong Kong,
so it attracts people trying to win.

The challenge is to estimate each horse's probability of winning.

Unlike dice or cards, racing is not just an idealized set of rules.
A six-sided die with equal probabilities can be defined analytically.
Horse racing involves real, individual horses and real-world phenomena.
It is a departure from pure mathematics.

To win at racing, we need a model to estimate those probabilities.


12. DESIRED CHARACTERISTICS OF A RACING MODEL
-------------------------------------------
A useful model should:

1. Combine heterogeneous variables into an overall predictor of
   horse performance.
2. Produce each horse's win probability as its output.
3. Produce probabilities summing to one within each race.
4. Have parameters that can practically be estimated from real data.

The sum-to-one condition is a common-sense constraint: in the ordinary
race illustration, there is one winning horse.

[Editorial scope: actual dead heats need a separately defined outcome
and settlement convention. They do not disappear merely because a
model normalizes probabilities.]


13. EXPECTED PERFORMANCE AS A WEIGHTED SUM OF FEATURES
----------------------------------------------------
We could view a horse's expected performance as the result of several
factors related to its past performances and today's race conditions.

One example specification could include:

- Average finishing position in past races.
- Days since the last race.
- Weight carried.
- The jockey's win percentage.
- Other characteristics of the horse or the race.

Each variable is multiplied by a coefficient. There could be perhaps
a hundred such characteristics.

The general form is:

  V_i = sum_k theta_k*x_ik,

where V_i represents the expected performance of horse i, x_ik the
predictor variables, and theta_k the model coefficients.

[Editorial notation: the subscripts are normalized here for readability.
The original slide was not supplied.]


14. ACTUAL PERFORMANCE: EXPECTATION PLUS RANDOM ERROR
----------------------------------------------------
A horse's actual performance in a particular race is not deterministic.
Random events occur, and there may be unknown variables that affect it.

We can write actual performance as the deterministic component V plus
a random error epsilon:

  Actual performance_i = V_i + epsilon_i.

This is common in statistical modelling.

If we assume epsilon is normally distributed, we obtain a normal
performance distribution around a mean.

The graph illustrates expected running time. We might think a horse
will run a race in just under 90 seconds, but its actual time will
vary around that because of unknown factors we cannot predict.


15. AN ENTIRE RACE: OVERLAPPING DISTRIBUTIONS
-------------------------------------------
Now consider an entire race, perhaps ten horses, or eight in the
particular illustration.

The graph shows overlapping normal distributions of possible running
times. I have intentionally made their widths different.

Some horses are more consistent and have a narrower distribution.
Other horses are less certain in their performance and have a wider
standard deviation.

This set of overlapping predicted running times is the problem we
really have in horse racing.

It leads to what is called a probit model of horse racing. A horse's
chance of winning is the probability that its performance is the
leftmost - the shortest running time - among all those distributions.
The equation at the bottom expresses that probability, but it is
rather complicated.

[Editorial terminology: different error variances are described as
heteroscedasticity. That is different from merely giving each horse

The supplied transcript does not specify the exact variance model,

16. ESTIMATING PARAMETERS THROUGH RACE WINNER LIKELIHOOD
------------------------------------------------------
Given that model, we can construct a likelihood for past races.

For a series of, say, 10,000 races, the likelihood of the observed
winners is the product of the probabilities assigned to the horses
that actually won:

  L(theta) = product_r P_theta(winner_r | field_r).

This produces a difficult estimation problem, requiring a complex
iterative method.

A professor at the Chinese University of Hong Kong has pioneered one
way of solving it. We have personally used his methodology to solve
this problem in practice.

[Name unresolved: the supplied transcript renders the professor's name
as "guming GAA." A reliable spelling was not supplied. Do not cite a
specific researcher or method on that spelling alone.]

I showed this to give a flavor of the work being done.

[Editorial connection to this repo: minimizing race winner log loss is
equivalent to maximizing the associated winner likelihood, subject to
any regularization and aggregation conventions. It is the training/
evaluation criterion, not a prediction target called "log loss."
This paragraph is an explanatory addition, not a speaker quotation.]

18. CONCLUSION: GAMBLING AND MATHEMATICS
--------------------------------------
A writer of one of the best books on mathematics and gambling concluded
that gamblers can rightfully claim to be the godfathers of probability
theory: their questions provoked the interaction between gambling and
mathematics that stimulated the study of probability.

I think that is true, particularly for the early history in the 1600s
and 1700s.

Today, gamblers are more often beneficiaries than the main inspiration
for mathematical innovation. Other needs drive mathematics, but keen
gamblers apply its techniques back to gambling, sometimes profitably.

There are a few references. Thank you.

19.1. APPLICATION TO THE STOCK MARKET

Moderator: We will entertain one or two questions.

Audience: Can these strategies be applied to the stock market?

Benter: Yes. Horse racing is probably one of the closest analogies.
One way of profiting in markets is to predict how a company's shares
or a currency will fare. This often involves modelling the underlying
real-world instrument from past data, much as we model a racehorse's
performance.

Racing is particularly amenable to statistical modelling because the
same sort of event occurs thousands of times. Financial markets often
contain more unique situations.

[Editorial scope: this is a comparison made in the talk, not a claim
that horse-racing outcomes or market conditions are perfectly stationary.]

19.2. DO BOOKMAKERS USE THE SAME MODELS?

Audience: Are bookmakers using the same sort of models to set the odds?

Benter: Generally, bookmakers act as intermediaries and respond to
how people are betting. They set odds to retain an edge and limit risk;
they act somewhat like toll collectors.

Hong Kong is different: there are not individual bookmakers operating
at the track in the sense described. Betting is organized through a
pari-mutuel system, with a fixed percentage withdrawn from the pools.

In that arrangement, it is the gamblers using these models, rather
than the racetrack setting a fixed price from such a model.

[Editorial scope: this summarizes the speaker's answer. It is not an
independently verified description of all modern bookmakers, their
modelling practices, regulation or every source of operator risk.]

19.3. CHANGING ODDS, EXPECTATION AND WHEN TO BET

Audience: Hong Kong racing odds can change shortly before the race.
After calculating a horse's probability, how do you calculate expected
value when the odds change? What is the optimal time to bet?

Benter: That is a good and astute question.

Once you know the probability of a horse winning, you also need to
know what the payoff will be. The probability of winning multiplied
by the amount won, plus the probability of losing multiplied by the
amount lost, gives the expectation.

If the payoff is unknown or changes at the last minute, it creates
uncertainty. A bet that appeared to have a positive expectation can
become negative if the odds drop.

The idea is to place the bet as late as possible so that your estimate
of the final payoff odds is as accurate as possible.

People generally build in a cushion for the possibility that odds
will fall late, particularly on horses likely to win. It is a problem
you have to deal with, and it does reduce profits.

Moderator: Are there any more questions? Thank you very much.

[Editorial scope: the talk's "last minute" suggestion is not a
guarantee of final odds or a live operational instruction. A system
still needs verified quote timestamps, submission/cutoff rules,
latency handling and uncertainty about the final dividend.]
